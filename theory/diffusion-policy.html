Diffusion Policy

First-principles derivation of visuomotor policy learning via action diffusion

Horizon Clarity

Denoising is learning — corrupt action sequences with noise, then train a neural net to reverse the corruption.

This is the sun-setting truth of diffusion policy. Everything else — the U-Net, the schedulers, the FiLM layers — is engineering. The fundamental insight is that by learning to undo a known corruption process, you implicitly learn the distribution of valid actions.

The Core Intuition

Imagine you have a collection of expert robot trajectories — sequences of joint positions that successfully pick up objects. These trajectories live in some complex, multimodal distribution. You want to sample from this distribution at test time.

The diffusion approach: instead of learning the distribution directly (hard), learn how to clean up a noisy version of it (easy). Start from pure noise, and iteratively denoise until you have a valid action sequence.

Key Insight

Noise → signal is equivalent to random → structured. Each denoising step adds a small amount of "structure" to the action sequence. After enough steps, random noise becomes a coherent manipulation trajectory.

The Forward Process (Corruption)

Given a clean action sequence $x_0$ from our expert demonstrations, the forward process progressively adds Gaussian noise over $T$ timesteps:

Derivation: Forward Diffusion

At each step $t$, add a small amount of noise:

$$q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}(x_t;\; \sqrt{1-\beta_t}\, x_{t-1},\; \beta_t I)$$

where $\beta_t$ is a variance schedule (small values, e.g. $\beta_1 = 10^{-4}$, $\beta_T = 0.02$).

The beautiful shortcut: We can jump directly to any timestep $t$ without iterating:

$$q(x_t \mid x_0) = \mathcal{N}(x_t;\; \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0,\; (1-\bar\alpha_t) I)$$

where $\alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t$ and $\bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s=1}^{t} \alpha_s$.

Reparameterization trick:

$$x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\, \epsilon, \quad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$$

As $t \to T$, $\bar\alpha_T \to 0$, so $x_T \approx \epsilon$ — pure noise.

Why this matters for robotics

The forward process defines a smooth path from "structured expert trajectory" to "random noise." The neural network's job is to learn to walk backwards along this path. The smoothness of the $\beta_t$ schedule ensures each backward step is a simple, learnable transformation.

The Reverse Process (Denoising)

We want to learn $p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$ — how to remove noise. The true reverse is intractable, but for small $\beta_t$, it's approximately Gaussian:

Derivation: Reverse Process

$$p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t) = \mathcal{N}(x_{t-1};\; \mu_\theta(x_t, t),\; \sigma_t^2 I)$$

The key insight (Ho et al., 2020): instead of predicting $\mu_\theta$ directly, predict the noise $\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)$ that was added. Then recover the mean via:

$$\mu_\theta(x_t, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\left(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\,\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\right)$$

Intuition: Predicting "what noise was added" is equivalent to predicting "what the clean signal looks like." The network answers: "Given this noisy thing, what part of it is just noise?"

Training Objective

The loss is stunningly simple — just predict the noise:

The Loss Function

$$\mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{t \sim U[1,T],\; x_0 \sim q,\; \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)}\Big[\|\epsilon - \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\|^2\Big]$$

In plain English:

  1. Sample a clean action $x_0$ from demonstrations
  2. Sample a random timestep $t$
  3. Add noise: $x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\, \epsilon$
  4. Predict the noise: $\hat\epsilon = \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)$
  5. Loss = MSE between true noise and predicted noise

That's it. That's the entire training procedure.

Why MSE and not something fancier? The variational lower bound (ELBO) derivation shows that the optimal denoising objective decomposes into independent per-timestep MSE terms. Ho et al. showed empirically that the simplified MSE works as well or better than the full weighted ELBO objective.

DDPM vs DDIM

Two ways to run the reverse process at inference time:

DDPM (Stochastic)

Add noise at each step — faithful to the probabilistic formulation but requires all $T$ steps (e.g., 100):

$$x_{t-1} = \mu_\theta(x_t, t) + \sigma_t\, z, \quad z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$$

DDIM (Deterministic)

Skip the stochastic term — enables fewer steps (e.g., 10 steps instead of 100):

$$x_{t-1} = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\hat{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)$$

where $\hat{x}_0 = \frac{x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}}$ is the predicted clean sample.

For robotics: use DDIM

Robots need fast inference (10-50Hz control). DDIM with 10 steps gives essentially the same quality as DDPM with 100 steps, but 10× faster. Our implementation uses DDIM with 10 inference steps.

Why Diffusion for Robot Actions?

Why not just regress actions directly with MSE? Or use a VAE? Or a GAN? The answer lies in multimodality.

The Multimodality Problem

Consider picking up a mug. You can grasp it by the handle (from the left), by the body (from the top), or by the rim. All are valid. The action distribution is multimodal.

Why MSE fails on multimodal data

If the true distribution has two modes at $a_1$ and $a_2$:

$$\arg\min_a \mathbb{E}\left[\|a - a^*\|^2\right] = \frac{a_1 + a_2}{2}$$

MSE regression outputs the mean — which is between the two valid actions and is itself not valid. The robot reaches halfway and does nothing useful.

Diffusion models naturally generate samples from multimodal distributions. During one rollout, the stochastic initialization commits to one mode and stays there — no averaging between incompatible actions.

Action Chunking

Instead of predicting one action $a_t$, predict a chunk of $H$ future actions $[a_t, a_{t+1}, \ldots, a_{t+H-1}]$.

Why chunks work

Temporal consistency: One denoising pass produces an entire trajectory segment. All actions in the chunk are consistent with each other — no jitter between independently predicted steps.

The numbers:

  • $H = 16$ — prediction horizon (generate 16 future actions)
  • $k = 8$ — execution horizon (execute only the first 8, then re-plan)
  • $T_o = 2$ — observation horizon (condition on last 2 observations)

Computational benefit: One forward pass of DDIM (10 denoising steps) produces 8 actions. Without chunking, you'd need one DDIM pass per action. That's 8× less compute for the same control rate.

Receding Horizon Control

Execute $k$ actions from the chunk, then re-plan with fresh observations. This creates a feedback loop: 

while not done:
    # Observe
    obs = get_observation()  # last T_o frames

    # Plan — one DDIM pass produces H actions
    action_chunk = policy.predict_action(obs)  # shape: (H, action_dim)

    # Execute only first k actions
    for a in action_chunk[:k]:
        env.step(a)

The trade-off

Large $k$ = fewer re-plans, faster but less reactive to perturbations.
Small $k$ = frequent re-plans, slower but handles disturbances.

We use $k = H/2 = 8$. Adjacent chunks overlap by 8 steps, providing continuity while staying reactive.

1D U-Net Backbone

The noise prediction network $\epsilon_\theta$ is a 1D convolutional U-Net. The input is the noisy action sequence (shape: action_dim × horizon), treated as a 1D signal with action_dim channels.

Architecture Details 
# Input: (B, action_dim, horizon) = (B, 4, 16)
# Conditioning: obs_embed (B, 256) + morph_cond (B, 64)
# Output: (B, action_dim, horizon) = predicted noise

ConditionalUnet1D(
    action_dim=4,          # joint positions
    cond_dim=256,          # obs encoder output
    morph_dim=64,          # morphology + task
    down_dims=[256, 512, 1024],
    kernel_size=5,
)

The U-Net has skip connections between encoder and decoder at each resolution. FiLM conditioning injects morphology information at every layer (see Week 3).

Observation Conditioning

The policy is conditioned on observations — it doesn't generate actions in a vacuum. The observation embedding enters the U-Net as a global conditioning vector:

$$\text{cond} = \text{obs\_encoder}(I_{1:T_o},\, p_{1:T_o})$$

where $I$ are RGB images (processed by ResNet-18) and $p$ is proprioception (joint positions/velocities). Both are stacked over the observation horizon $T_o = 2$ to provide temporal context.

Timestep Embedding

The network must know "how noisy is the input?" — this is encoded via sinusoidal position embeddings (same technique as Transformers):

$$\text{emb}(t) = [\sin(t/10000^{0/d}),\, \cos(t/10000^{0/d}),\, \ldots,\, \sin(t/10000^{(d-1)/d})]$$

The timestep embedding is added to the conditioning vector at each U-Net layer, telling the network whether to make large corrections (high $t$, very noisy) or fine adjustments (low $t$, nearly clean).

Full Algorithm

Training

for batch in dataloader:
    obs, actions = batch          # actions: (B, H, action_dim)
    actions = actions.permute(0, 2, 1)  # → (B, action_dim, H) for Conv1D

    # 1. Encode observations
    obs_embed = obs_encoder(obs)

    # 2. Sample random timestep
    t = randint(0, T, size=(B,))

    # 3. Add noise
    noise = randn_like(actions)
    noisy_actions = sqrt(alpha_bar[t]) * actions + sqrt(1 - alpha_bar[t]) * noise

    # 4. Predict noise
    noise_pred = unet(noisy_actions, t, obs_embed, morph_cond)

    # 5. MSE loss
    loss = mse(noise_pred, noise)
    loss.backward()
    optimizer.step()

Inference (DDIM)

def predict_action(obs, morph_vec):
    obs_embed = obs_encoder(obs)

    # Start from pure noise
    x = randn(B, action_dim, H)

    # Iteratively denoise (10 steps)
    for t in ddim_timesteps:  # e.g., [999, 888, ..., 111, 0]
        noise_pred = unet(x, t, obs_embed, morph_cond)

        # DDIM update step
        x0_pred = (x - sqrt(1-alpha_bar[t]) * noise_pred) / sqrt(alpha_bar[t])
        x = sqrt(alpha_bar[t-1]) * x0_pred + sqrt(1-alpha_bar[t-1]) * noise_pred

    return x.permute(0, 2, 1)[:, :k, :]  # first k actions

Practical Tips

What actually matters in practice

  1. Action normalization: Normalize actions to $[-1, 1]$. The noise schedule assumes unit-scale data.
  2. EMA: Use exponential moving average of weights (decay=0.995). Smooths training noise.
  3. Gradient clipping: Clip at norm 1.0. Prevents divergence on rare high-loss batches.
  4. Cosine schedule > linear: Cosine preserves more signal at low timesteps, giving better fine-grained control predictions.
  5. Don't clip actions during diffusion: Let the U-Net output go beyond $[-1,1]$ during intermediate steps. Only clip the final output.
